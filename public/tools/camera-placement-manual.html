01Get a first result

Five minutes from opening the file to a defensible answer.

  1. Describe the road Set Lanes and Lane width. The scene rebuilds as you drag. Road width is simply lanes × lane width.
  2. Place the camera Set Height and Lateral X. X = 0 puts the camera over the road centre (a gantry); move it sideways for a roadside pole. The badge under the controls tells you which one you've built.
  3. Aim it Set Working distance — how far down the road you care about — then press Re-center on road. That computes the tilt and pan which point the lens at the road centre at that distance. Adjust from there.
  4. Enter the real lens and sensor Type the Horizontal FOV from the lens datasheet and the Sensor resolution from the camera datasheet. Leave the vertical FOV locked (🔒) so it follows the sensor shape.
  5. Pick the plate Choose a Plate type preset, or enter a custom size in inches.
  6. Read the answer The Per-lane list gives plate width × height and character height in pixels for every lane. Compare against your ANPR engine's requirement — commonly 80–130 px plate width, or 16–20 px character height.
  7. Let the solver finish the job Open Autocompute, enter your pixel and speed targets, tick the parameters you're willing to change, and press Solve.
Orientation

Traffic travels away from the camera along +Z. Lane 1 is always the lane nearest the side the camera is mounted on, so the numbering flips automatically when you move the camera across the road.

02The 3D scene

Two cameras exist. Don't confuse them.

The large 3D view is your orbit camera — drag to rotate, scroll to zoom, right-drag to pan. Moving it changes nothing about the design; it's just how you look at the model.

The Camera sensor view panel is the real thing: exactly what the camera being designed sees, rendered at the sensor's aspect ratio through the tilt, pan and FOV you've set. Click it to enlarge, and use Save PNG to export at full sensor resolution.

Road markings labelled LANE 1, LANE 2… sit painted on the tarmac near the camera. They are deliberately excluded from the sensor view so they can't be mistaken for something the camera would actually record.

03Road & camera

Every slider value can also be typed — click the number beside a field to edit it directly.

Lanes1 – 6 · step 1

How many lanes the camera must cover. Each lane is evaluated independently; results are reported for the worst one.

Lane width2.5 – 4.5 m · step 0.1

Centre-to-centre lane spacing. 3.5 m is a typical motorway lane; 3.0–3.3 m is common in dense urban layouts.

Height1 – 12 m · step 0.1

Mounting height above the road surface. Higher reduces the risk of vehicles occluding one another, but steepens the view angle onto the plate, which shortens it in the image.

Lateral Xacross the road ±4 m · step 0.1

Sideways offset from the road centreline. Zero is an overhead gantry. The slider range grows automatically with the road width.

This also sets lane numbering: positive X numbers lanes right-to-left, negative numbers them left-to-right, so Lane 1 is always nearest the camera.

Tilt — down angle0 – 89° · step 0.1

How far the lens is pitched below horizontal. 0° stares at the horizon; large values look steeply down at the road directly below.

Pan — aim L/R−90 – 90° · step 0.1

Horizontal rotation. Needed whenever the camera sits off to one side and must swing back across the carriageway.

Working distance0 – 150 m · step 1

The down-road distance you're designing for. It positions the sample vehicles and is the distance Re-center on road aims at. It does not itself constrain the readable zone — that comes out of the geometry.

Re-center on road

Computes tilt and pan that point the optical axis at the road centre, at plate height, at the current working distance. It's the fastest way out of a nonsensical aim, and a good starting point before running the solver.

04Lens & field of view

Horizontal and vertical FOV are not independent — the sensor shape ties them together.

Beside each FOV value is a padlock. Locking one axis derives it from the other plus the sensor resolution, which is the physically correct behaviour for a normal camera with square pixels. The locked slider greys out; the driving one stays live.

  • 🔒 on vertical — you set hFOV, vFOV follows. This is the usual choice, since lens datasheets quote horizontal FOV.
  • 🔒 on horizontal — the reverse, for datasheets that quote vertical.
  • Both unlocked — H and V set independently. Only correct for anamorphic optics or non-square pixels.

Locking is exclusive: locking one releases the other. Click an active padlock again to release it.

Vertical FOV when locked vFOV = 2 · atan( tan(hFOV / 2) · SH / SW )

The Diagonal figure below the sliders is computed, never entered — it exists because many datasheets quote diagonal FOV and you'll want to cross-check.

Don't do this

Diagonal FOV is not √(H² + V²). That angle-space shortcut is only valid for narrow fields; at 25–30° it is already off by more than half a degree, and at wide angles it is badly wrong. The tool uses the tangent form shown in section 09.

Typing exact values

Sliders step in 0.1°. To enter a datasheet value precisely, click the number itself — it becomes editable. Press Enter to commit or Esc to cancel. Values outside the valid range are clamped to the nearest limit.

05Zoom lenses

Model a varifocal lens by declaring both ends of its range.

Tick Zoom lens. The FOV you currently have becomes the wide end, and a second pair of fields appears for the tele end — the narrowest the lens goes. Both ends respect the same padlock, so the vertical figures stay consistent with your sensor.

With zoom active, the hFOV and vFOV sliders become the zoom position. A shaded band on each track marks the lens's real range, and the thumb will not move past either edge. The readout shows where you are:

Zoom readout 2.31× of 4.23× max  (wide 29.4° → tele 7.1°)
Optical zoom factor zoom = tan(FOVwide / 2) / tan(FOVtele / 2)

The factor is identical whether measured horizontally or vertically — the sensor aspect cancels — so a single number describes the lens.

Redefining the wide end

The wide bound is captured at the moment you tick the box, so it stays fixed while you zoom. To change it, untick Zoom lens, set the new wide FOV, and tick it again.

When the solver is allowed to vary FOV, it searches only within the lens's tele–wide range and reports the zoom factor needed for the solution it found — a figure you can take straight to a procurement spec.

06Sensor & plate

Sensor resolutionpixels, W × H

The camera's actual output resolution. Everything measured in pixels depends on this, and it drives the locked FOV axis. Use the real streaming resolution, not the sensor's maximum, if they differ.

Plate typeUS · EU · Indian · Custom

Presets fill in standard plate dimensions and change the rendered plate's appearance in the 3D scene.

Plate sizeinches, W × H

Editing either figure switches the preset to Custom. Physical plate size directly scales the pixel result — get this right for the region you're installing in.

Plate mount height0 – 2 m · step 0.05

Height of the plate centre above the road. 0.5 m suits most cars; raise it for trucks and buses. The difference between camera height and this value is what sets the vertical geometry.

Approach angle−60 – 60° · step 1

Yaw of the vehicle relative to the lane direction — for vehicles crossing at an angle, or plates on a curve. Non-zero values foreshorten the plate and reduce readable width.

Char / plate height ratio0.3 – 0.95 · step 0.01

What fraction of the plate's height the characters occupy. Used to convert plate height into character height, since many ANPR specifications are written in character pixels.

Plate imageoptional upload

Drop in a photo of a real plate to see it mapped onto the vehicles. Use synthetic plate returns to the generated one.

Per-lane readout

For each lane: plate width × height in pixels, and character height (prefixed c). A green dot means the plate is in frame at the working distance; grey means it isn't.

How pixels are measured

Plate height is the distance between the midpoints of the top and bottom edges — the projected centreline, not a bounding box. Under a skewed view a bounding box over-reports by up to a factor of two, which would quietly flatter every result.

Camera sensor view

The true camera image. If a plate isn't visible here, no amount of pixel arithmetic elsewhere matters — it isn't in frame. Click to enlarge and save at sensor resolution.

08Autocompute solver

Fix what's constrained by the site. Let the tool work out the rest.

Targets

FieldWhat it does
Required plate pixelsThe pixel threshold a plate must meet to count as readable.
MeasureWhether that threshold applies to plate width or to character height. Match whichever your ANPR vendor specifies.
Vehicle speedDesign speed in km/h, used for the capture guarantee.
Camera frame rateFrames per second actually delivered by the camera.
Cover full lane widthOn: a plate must be readable anywhere across the lane, not just on the centreline. Leave this on for honest results.

Free parameters

Tick anything the solver may change: Height, Lateral X, Tilt, Pan, H-FOV, V-FOV, Lane width, Mount height. Everything unticked is held at its current value. A FOV axis that is padlocked appears greyed — it's derived, so freeing it would mean nothing.

Constraints and objective

The two constraints are pass/fail floors:

  • Plate pixels ≥ required — the worst lane must meet your pixel threshold.
  • Capture guaranteed — the readable zone must be long enough that a vehicle at the design speed cannot pass through between frames.

The objective is what gets maximised within those floors:

ObjectiveMaximisesUse when
max pixelsWorst-lane plate pixelsYou want the sharpest possible plate.
max speedReadable zone lengthYou want the widest speed tolerance.
max lanesNumber of lanes coveredYou're asking how much road one camera can serve.

max lanes sweeps 1 through 6 lanes, optimising your free parameters at each count, and reports the largest that still satisfies every constraint — with a table showing exactly where it breaks down.

Reading the result

A green FEASIBLE pill means every enabled constraint is satisfied; the solved values are applied to the scene immediately so you can inspect them. INFEASIBLE means nothing in the allowed range works — what's shown is the closest the solver could get, which is useful for seeing how far off you are.

The report lists worst-lane pixels, readable zone length, guaranteed max speed, readable frames at your design speed, the zoom factor needed, and the readable range for every lane.

Tiebreaks

Where many solutions score identically, the solver prefers the sensible one: on equal pixels it takes the longer readable zone, and on otherwise equal results it takes the lower mounting height. These nudges are deliberately tiny and can never override a real gain in the objective.

09The maths

Everything the tool reports, and where it comes from.

Scene axes

+X right, +Y up, +Z down the road. The camera sits at (X, height, 0); traffic occupies positive Z. Lane i (counting from zero) has its centre at:

laneX = −roadW / 2 + (i + 0.5) · laneW   where  roadW = lanes · laneW

Lane numbering

A display convention only — it never changes the physics, which always takes the worst lane.

label = (camX > 0) ? lanes − i : i + 1

Projection

A rectilinear pinhole. From tilt and pan, build the forward vector, then the right and up vectors, and project a world point into normalised device coordinates:

Basis f = [ sin(pan)·cos(tilt), −sin(tilt), cos(pan)·cos(tilt) ]
r = normalize( f × up )    u = r × f
Point to pixels zc = v · f   (depth along the axis; behind the camera ⇒ not visible)
ndx = (v · r / zc) / tan(hFOV / 2)
ndy = (v · u / zc) / tan(vFOV / 2)
px  = ( (ndx·0.5 + 0.5) · SW,  (1 − (ndy·0.5 + 0.5)) · SH )

The point is in frame when both ndx and ndy fall within −1…1.

Field of view relationships

Vertical from horizontal (square pixels) vFOV = 2 · atan( tan(hFOV / 2) · SH / SW )
Diagonal — from the sensor rectangle, not the angles D = 2 · atan( √( tan²(H / 2) + tan²(V / 2) ) )

Both fall out of the same pinhole triangle: half-width = f·tan(H/2), half-height = f·tan(V/2). Pythagoras applies on the sensor, in millimetres — never on the angles. The focal length cancels, which is why only the aspect ratio is needed.

Plate size in pixels

hPx   = | midpoint(TL,TR) − midpoint(BL,BR) |
wPx   = | midpoint(TL,BL) − midpoint(TR,BR) |
charPx = hPx · charRatio

Readable zone and capture

A plate is readable where it is both in frame and meets the pixel threshold. That produces a zone from readNear to readFar, of length L. With full-width coverage on, the tool samples across the whole lane and keeps the worst offset; it then takes the worst lane.

Capture guarantee — the vehicle cannot skip the zone L ≥ speed / fps   (speed in m/s)
maxSpeed = L · fps · 3.6  km/h
frames   = floor( L · fps / (speed / 3.6) )

Solver scoring

Feasible candidates are ranked by the objective plus the tiebreaks; infeasible ones by how badly they miss, so the search still converges toward the closest achievable design.

Fitness · max pixels feasible  : worstPeak + 0.001·worstL − 0.0001·camH
infeasible: −10⁶ − violation · 1000

The scale is deliberate: one pixel of real gain outweighs the entire zone tiebreak, which in turn outweighs the height tiebreak. Ties are broken sensibly; genuine improvements are never overridden.

Nothing is stored in the browser. A configuration exists only as a file you exported, which means it can be version-controlled, attached to a tender, or emailed to whoever signs off the install.

11Things to watch

Very wide lenses

The whole model assumes a rectilinear lens. Beyond roughly 90–100° horizontal, most real lenses are fisheye — equidistant or equisolid — and neither the derived vertical FOV nor the pixel figures will match the hardware. Check the datasheet's own vertical figure; if it disagrees materially with the derived one, the lens isn't rectilinear.

Flat optima

Some geometries genuinely under-determine a parameter. A centred gantry at long range, for instance, can be almost insensitive to tilt — many values give the same pixel count. The solver's tiebreaks pick a reasonable member of that set, but if a solved value looks arbitrary, this is usually why. Compare the readable range against the aim to confirm it's physically sensible.

Pixels can saturate

Under full-width coverage, zooming in shrinks the horizontal field and pushes the near edge of the readable zone further out, so worst-lane pixels can plateau instead of rising. When that happens, extra zoom buys zone length rather than sharpness — which is exactly what the zone tiebreak is there to capture.

Model limits

Not modelled: motion blur and shutter speed, lens distortion, focus and depth of field, IR illumination and reflectivity, plate dirt or damage, headlight glare, weather, or vehicles occluding one another. Treat the pixel figures as a geometric ceiling — real-world performance sits below it.

Sanity check before you commit

Open the sensor view and confirm the plate is visibly in frame at the near and far ends of the reported readable zone. If the geometry says a range is readable but the camera clearly isn't looking there, trust the picture and re-check tilt, FOV and the padlock state.
